ANA

Discipline is not a choice, when it is the only thing that still responds when everything else fails.

Ana is a platform diver — a body sculpted by repetition, by precision, by absolute control over every movement. In Ceres, this is not just a sport: it is a function, a guaranteed place within the machinery, a visible proof of utility. And Ana fulfills this function with a discipline that never seems to waver — at least on the outside.

She was Daime's partner before the Flagball accident that left him in a wheelchair. What tore them apart afterward was not a lack of love — it was the cold logic of function. Daime, feeling incapable of "fulfilling the function of a partner" within a system that measures value by productive capacity, dismisses her — not because he stopped feeling something, but because he hates himself too much to stay close to her while being, in his own eyes, incomplete. Ana still likes him. But love, in Ceres, is never enough when the machinery demands something else.

What she finds later, with Dru, is not a substitute for affection — it is pleasure without feeling, traded almost like a commodity: lipstick, perfume, a moment of physical contact without any emotional demands. Ana does not like Dru. The relationship is purely physical, a way to fill a void, without allowing herself vulnerability again. It is telling that the only form of intimacy she allows herself, after losing Daime to the logic of function, is precisely the one that requires no function at all — just exchange, just the body, with no risk of disappointing or being dismissed again.

And then there is the Girl Ceres — rival, mirror, adversary. Both are elite athletes, competing not just for a medal or a position, but for symbolic leadership among thousands of military academies scattered across Ceres. It is a silent, psychological war, fought in the pool and on the platform, where every dive is also a gesture of dominance. Ana is perhaps the only person in history who understands, in her own body, what it means to compete for emotional survival through performance — the same logic that governs the Girl Ceres in her search for belonging, only applied here to another wound: needing to win to prove she is still worth something after having been discarded for no longer serving someone she loved.

Ana is, perhaps, the most silent portrait of how Ceres transforms loss into performance: she does not cry for Daime in public, she does not beg, she does not allow herself visible weakness. She jumps. Again. And again. Each perfect dive is, at the same time, genuine discipline and grief disguised as excellence.
